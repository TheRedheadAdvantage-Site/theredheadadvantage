How to get important work conversations in writing

If a conversation affects expectations, ownership, deadlines, performance, or a decision that could matter later, a short written follow-up can protect clarity without making you look defensive.

By Charlotte, founder of The Redhead Advantage

A lot of workplace problems are not caused by one dramatic conversation. They happen because two people leave the same conversation interpreting or remembering different things.

A deadline may have been moved, but nobody wrote down the new date. A manager gave verbal feedback, then months later described the issue differently. You agreed to take on one piece of work, and somehow the expectation became the entire project.

I am a big believer in getting important work conversations in writing. It doesn’t need to be every conversation or every tiny request, but the ones that could affect your performance, workload, reputation, compensation, or ability to explain what happened later should always be documented.

The easiest way to do this is usually not to announce that you are documenting something. Just send a normal follow-up email.

After a meeting, you might write: “Thanks for the conversation. Just confirming that I’ll own X, Finance will deliver Y by Thursday, and we’re targeting Monday for the final review.”

If the conversation was about feedback, you can do the same thing: “I want to make sure I’m clear on the expectation. The two areas you want me to focus on over the next 30 days are X and Y, and we’ll check in again on Friday the 18th.”

The point is not to build a case against your manager. The point is to create one shared version of what was agreed.

When to put it in writing

I would be especially deliberate when the conversation involves deadlines, performance concerns, changes in scope, ownership between teams, compensation, promotion expectations, leave, or a decision that could later be attributed to you.

If something in a written recap is wrong, correct it promptly and neutrally. You do not need a long argument. State the fact, the agreed change, and the next step.

Good documentation should make work easier, not more adversarial. Keep it factual, short, and write it as though a perfectly reasonable third person might read it later.

You do not need to document your entire working life, but when the stakes are high enough that you would care if the story changed later, give yourself a written record.

I used to think that if I did excellent work, the right people would notice. Sometimes they did, but doing good work and being seen as ready for a bigger role are two different things.

Most organizations don’t promote people simply because they’re busy, dependable, or overdue for recognition. They promote people when they believe that person can solve a problem at the next level.

That means your work has to be visible in the right way, and your manager needs to understand the decisions you made, the results they produced, and the kind of responsibility you’re ready to take on. If all they see is someone who quietly handles everything, they may value you enormously in your current role without imagining you in a different one.

This doesn’t mean you need to talk about yourself constantly, but be more deliberate in the work you’re doing, and who you’re getting that work in front of.

When you’re looking to move up, ask what the next role actually requires, find out who makes the decision and what evidence they look for, then make sure your work gives them that evidence.

A useful question to take into your next conversation with your manager is: “What would you need to see from me to feel confident recommending me for the next level?”

Listen carefully to the answer. If it’s specific, you have something to work with. If it stays vague after repeated conversations, that tells you something too.

Working hard still matters, but you deserve to know whether the work you’re doing is moving you toward the career you want.
