Taking over a failing project? Ask these questions first

When you inherit a late or struggling project, moving faster is not always the first answer. Before you own the recovery, get clear on the original commitment, what changed, who owns what, and which tradeoffs are actually available.

By Charlotte, founder of The Redhead Advantage

Getting handed a late project can make you feel like you need to prove yourself by immediately fixing it.

That instinct can create a bigger problem: you start owning commitments you never made, timelines you never agreed to, and dependencies you do not control.

Before you promise a recovery plan, understand what you are actually inheriting.

What was originally promised?

Start with the baseline. What was the original deadline, scope, budget, and definition of success? Who made those commitments?

Then ask what has changed since that commitment was made. Scope may have grown. A dependency may have slipped. A decision may have been delayed. The original plan may simply have been unrealistic.

What is actually causing the delay?

Do not accept “we’re behind” as the diagnosis. Find the constraint.

Is the team short on capacity? Is a decision waiting on leadership? Is another team late? Is the technical solution failing? Are requirements still changing?

If you do not know the constraint, you cannot know which recovery action will work.

Who owns each dependency?

Make the ownership visible. Your plan should distinguish between work your team controls and work that requires another team or leader.

That matters because a recovery plan built around dependencies nobody has agreed to is not a recovery plan.

Which tradeoffs are available?

If the date cannot move, can scope move? If scope cannot move, can resources change? If neither can change, what risk is leadership consciously accepting?

Senior project leadership is often less about magically finding more hours and more about making the tradeoffs explicit.

Reset the record

Once you understand the situation, write down the new plan: what is changing, what is not, who owns each action, the dates, the risks, and when you will review progress.

You can absolutely take ownership of fixing a struggling project. Just make sure you are taking ownership of the recovery, not silently inheriting every mistake that happened before you arrived.

I used to think that if I did excellent work, the right people would notice. Sometimes they did, but doing good work and being seen as ready for a bigger role are two different things.

Most organizations don’t promote people simply because they’re busy, dependable, or overdue for recognition. They promote people when they believe that person can solve a problem at the next level.

That means your work has to be visible in the right way, and your manager needs to understand the decisions you made, the results they produced, and the kind of responsibility you’re ready to take on. If all they see is someone who quietly handles everything, they may value you enormously in your current role without imagining you in a different one.

This doesn’t mean you need to talk about yourself constantly, but be more deliberate in the work you’re doing, and who you’re getting that work in front of.

When you’re looking to move up, ask what the next role actually requires, find out who makes the decision and what evidence they look for, then make sure your work gives them that evidence.

A useful question to take into your next conversation with your manager is: “What would you need to see from me to feel confident recommending me for the next level?”

Listen carefully to the answer. If it’s specific, you have something to work with. If it stays vague after repeated conversations, that tells you something too.

Working hard still matters, but you deserve to know whether the work you’re doing is moving you toward the career you want.
