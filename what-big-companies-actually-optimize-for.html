What big companies actually optimize for

Company decisions make more sense when you understand the goals, constraints, tradeoffs, and competing priorities behind them.

One of the most useful things I learned working across finance, operations, and ecommerce is that a company can make a decision that feels completely irrational from your seat and entirely logical from another.

Your team may be trying to improve the customer experience. Another team may be measured on cost. Leadership may be protecting this quarter’s results while also funding a longer-term bet. People can be doing their jobs well and still be pulling in different directions.

That’s why “this is a good idea” is rarely enough to get something approved. The question is: Good for which goal, at what cost, and compared with what else?

If you want support for an idea, start by understanding the decision the other person is actually making. What are they accountable for? What tradeoff worries them? What would they have to delay or give up to say yes to you?

Then make your case in those terms. Explain the outcome, the cost, the risk, and how you’ll know whether it worked. You don’t need to turn every conversation into a formal business case. You do need to show that you understand the broader picture.

This matters for your career, too. The people who advance often do more than execute their own work well. They learn to see the constraints around a decision and help others make a better one.

Once you understand what a company is optimizing for, its decisions become easier to interpret. You can advocate for your ideas more effectively, and decide more clearly whether the place you work is still aligned with what matters to you.
